The Upgrade Tree asks one question: how much of a human being can we change on purpose today? Not in a story, and not someday, but with a device, a drug or a procedure that at least one person has actually had, counted from the public record.

One idea about the future says that as machines get smarter, people keep up by joining with them: better senses, better memory, a direct line from brain to computer. Whether that is happening is a question with an answer, and the answer is a list. The site keeps that list in the open, so that anyone can check an entry, argue with a stage or add what is missing. It gives no odds: a stage, a date and a record say what has happened, and the site does not guess about what will.

This is the start of the site's news section. When a weekly reading moves an upgrade up a stage, or turns up a record that changes one, I will write it up here beside the entry it changes. A quiet week gets no post. An essay about the project is coming.

## Where the count stands

As of 18 October 2026, [28 of the 35 ways](/data/headline.json) of changing a human being on purpose that the site tracks have been tried in at least one living person. Each upgrade sits on one of five stages:

- idea only, not yet shown to work in any living animal: 4 ([the branches](/branches/));
- shown in animals, in a peer-reviewed report: 3;
- first human, meaning at least one person has had it, on the public record: 18;
- in clinical use, approved by a regulator somewhere or in routine use in a named clinic: 7;
- in wide use, available to the public in at least one country and taken up by many: 3.

The headline counts the last three. The 35 upgrades hang on [seven branches](/branches/): senses; movement and body; memory and thinking; mood and drive; lifespan and repair; inheritance; and the link to machines. The link to machines has seven upgrades, the most of any branch, and the site treats a direct link between a brain and a machine as the change that would matter most.

That branch carries the second number, how fast a brain can send information to a machine. The fastest peer-reviewed rate for turning intended speech into text is [78 words a minute](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06443-4), from the University of California, San Francisco, on 23 August 2023, about half the speed of talking, with one word in four wrong. [The newest point](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2314132), from the University of California, Davis, on 14 August 2024, is slower at 31.6 words a minute but gets only 2.5 words in a hundred wrong. For moving a cursor, the best peer-reviewed rate is [4.16 bits a second](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.18554), from Stanford in 2017. [Neuralink](https://neuralink.com/)'s [own figure of 8.0 bits a second](https://neuralink.com/updates/prime-study-progress-update-user-experience/), from May 2024, is on the chart marked as not peer reviewed.

Beside the count sits [a log of 27 claims](/claims/) about merging with machines, from 2000 to 2026, set against what the branches have actually reached.

## How to follow it

Every Sunday evening, Pacific time, a fresh session reads every upgrade's test against the past week's news and gives each a verdict. It may move an upgrade up one stage, and only on a primary record of the kind that upgrade names, never on press coverage, a company's claim, a plan or a demonstration video; anything else it leaves for me.

Each week's reading is on [the Reading page](https://theupgradetree.com/reading/), and [the Story](https://theupgradetree.com/story/) lists every change. [The Branches](https://theupgradetree.com/branches/) lead to every upgrade with its test and records, [the Claims](https://theupgradetree.com/claims/) page is the log of who said what, and the bandwidth chart is on [the link-to-machines branch](https://theupgradetree.com/branches/link/#bandwidth). [The Method](https://theupgradetree.com/method/) explains how the count is worked out and who does what. If you think an upgrade is missing or a stage or test is wrong, [the Disagree page](https://theupgradetree.com/disagree/) has a form for each, and I rule on every one. Every new post also goes out on [the news feed](https://theupgradetree.com/news/feed.xml).

## The most interesting news so far

The first weekly reading, on 16 October 2026, caught a mistake in an entry written that same day. The entry for hearing gene therapy said no regulator had approved one, but the United States Food and Drug Administration had given [accelerated approval](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-ever-gene-therapy-treatment-genetic-hearing-loss-under-national-priority-voucher) to [Regeneron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regeneron_Pharmaceuticals)'s Otarmeni, the first gene therapy for a genetic hearing loss, on 23 April 2026. The upgrade moved from first human to in clinical use. The headline stayed at 28, because the upgrade was already counted.

The oldest date on the tree may be about to get older. The upgrade "a headset worn on the scalp controls a device without surgery" dates its first human to December 1988, [a brain-controlled speller](https://doi.org/10.1016/0013-4694(88)90149-6) from the University of Illinois. The first reading found [a 1977 paper](https://doi.org/10.1109/PROC.1977.10542) by Jacques Vidal of the University of California, Los Angeles, described second-hand, and on 18 October 2026 the paper itself was read in full. In it, volunteers with electrodes on the scalp and no surgery steered a symbol through a simple maze on a screen, choosing each direction by fixing their gaze on one of four dots. The computer sorted the brain's response to each flash into one of four directions, and was right more than 90 percent of the time with average volunteers. A change moving the first human to May 1977 is waiting on my ruling. The question is whether choosing by where you look counts as the headset reading your brain. Either way, no count changes.

Building the tree also overturned some expected dates: the visual cortex implant's first human came in 1968 ([the story](/story/)), fifty years earlier than the site's plan had it, and a memory implant was first tried in a person in 2012, six years earlier.

The claims log has had its own corrections. The entry for the historian [Yuval Noah Harari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuval_Noah_Harari) said the word "hackable" never appears in his 2020 talk at Davos. Listening again found "We are now hackable animals" about twelve minutes in, where the automatic captions had heard "packable animals". [Neuralink's own home page](https://neuralink.com/), read on 16 October 2026, does not mention artificial intelligence, symbiosis or merging anywhere.

## What to watch for

The nearest result that could raise the count to 29 of 35 is in resetting cells to reverse ageing. [Life Biosciences](https://www.lifebiosciences.com/) dosed the first participant in its trial for two optic nerve diseases on 9 June 2026, and no result has been reported. The upgrade reaches first human only when a measured marker of age in a person moves towards youth.

For a brain link faster than speech, the test is a sustained rate above 40 bits a second for at least ten minutes, in a peer-reviewed report on a person. [Paradromics](https://www.paradromics.com/) claims more than 200 bits a second in a preprint and in animals, and reported a first permanent human implant in June 2026, with no human rate published yet.

Upgrades already counted can still climb towards clinical use. For organs from gene-edited pigs, trials cleared by the Food and Drug Administration are enrolling, and [Mass General Brigham](https://www.massgeneralbrigham.org/) has said a third transplant is planned later in 2026. For a deep brain implant for severe depression, [Abbott](https://www.abbott.com/)'s main trial, begun in September 2024, has not yet reported.

On the site's own record, the next change waiting is my ruling on [the 1977 paper](/branches/link/link-scalp-headset-controls-a-device-without-surgery/).
